The 65/20 rule: who studies 20 civics questions instead of 128

November 8, 2026 · 5 min read

Applicants aged 65 or over with 20 years as permanent residents take a shorter civics test in their own language. What the rule gives, what it leaves in place, and which twenty questions apply.

Most applicants for United States citizenship prepare for two tests: an English test, and a civics test drawn from a bank of 128 questions. A small group prepares for neither in that form. They study twenty questions, answer them in the language they speak at home, and skip the English test altogether.

The provision that allows this is known as the 65/20 rule. It is not an exception granted case by case. It is a fixed rule with two conditions, and anyone who meets them is entitled to it.

What the rule is

The 65/20 rule is the special consideration given to an applicant who is 65 years old or older and has been a lawful permanent resident for at least 20 years on the day Form N-400 is filed. Both conditions must be met, and both are measured on the filing date, not on the interview date.

The two numbers in the name are the two conditions: 65 years of age, 20 years of permanent residence.

What it changes

Three things.

  • The English test does not apply. An applicant who qualifies does not have to read, write or speak English for the purposes of the test.
  • The interview can be held in another language. The applicant chooses the language and brings an interpreter who is fluent in both that language and English. USCIS does not supply one.
  • The civics list is shorter. Instead of the full question bank, USCIS publishes a separate list of 20 questions, marked with an asterisk in the official materials. The officer asks 10 of those 20 at the interview, and 6 correct answers are a pass.

That last point is the one most often misread. The twenty questions are not a sample to practise on. They are the whole list: nothing outside them is asked.

What it does not change

The 65/20 rule is a shorter civics test, not the absence of one. Six correct answers out of ten are still required, and an applicant who does not reach six has the same second chance as everyone else.

The rest of the naturalization requirements are untouched as well: continuous residence, physical presence, good moral character, and the interview on the contents of Form N-400. The rule shortens one part of the examination and leaves the rest where it was.

The English test and the civics test are separate requirements. An exemption from the first says nothing about the second, and the 65/20 rule is the only provision that shortens both at once.

Where the other two rules fit

Two neighbouring rules use the same arithmetic and are easy to confuse with this one.

RuleAgeYears as permanent residentWhat it gives
50/2050 or over20 or moreNo English test; civics in the applicant's language, full question list
55/1555 or over15 or moreThe same as 50/20
65/2065 or over20 or moreThe same, and a civics list of 20 questions

Read side by side, the structure becomes clear. The first two rules remove the language barrier. The third removes it and shortens the material as well.

Anyone who meets 65/20 necessarily meets 50/20, since 65 is more than 50 and the 20 years are the same. The reverse does not hold: a 52-year-old with 22 years of residence is exempt from English but studies the full list.

Which twenty questions

Here the filing date matters. USCIS replaced the civics test in the autumn of 2025, and the version that applies to an application is determined by when Form N-400 was filed.

  • Filed before 20 October 2025: the 2008 test applies. The full bank is 100 questions; the officer asks up to 10 and 6 correct are a pass.
  • Filed on or after 20 October 2025: the 2025 test applies. The full bank is 128 questions; the officer asks up to 20 and 12 correct are a pass.

For an applicant under the 65/20 rule the shorter structure has carried over through every version of the test so far: twenty questions to study, ten asked, six to pass. What changes is which twenty, because the list is drawn from the bank in force. An applicant preparing from an old booklet may be studying the right number of questions from the wrong version, and that is the most common avoidable mistake in this part of the process.

The practical check is one line on the receipt notice: the filing date of Form N-400. Everything else follows from it.

Counting the twenty years

The twenty years are counted from the date on the permanent resident card, the one labelled «Resident Since», to the date the application is filed. Time spent abroad does not reset the count by itself, although long absences can raise separate questions about continuous residence.

An applicant who is a few months short of either threshold has a simple option: wait. Filing early does not preserve the benefit, because both conditions are read on the filing date. Filing a little later secures the shorter test, the interpreter and the exemption from English.

The twenty questions of the 65/20 list are the reason our study guide for applicants aged 65 and over prints only those twenty, in English and in Spanish, in extra large type, with the answer explained rather than merely stated. A list of twenty is short enough that each one deserves a page.
