The 2025 civics test: what changed, and which version applies

November 9, 2026 · 3 min read

Since 20 October 2025 two civics tests have been in use at the same time. The filing date on Form N-400 decides which one an applicant sits, and the two differ in bank size, questions asked and answers needed.

On 20 October 2025 USCIS replaced the civics test for naturalization. The new test is longer, the question bank is larger, and the number of correct answers needed to pass has doubled. Both versions are now in use at the same time, and which one an applicant sits depends on a single date.

The date that decides

The version follows the day Form N-400 was filed, not the day of the interview.

An application filed before 20 October 2025 is tested on the 2008 version. An application filed on or after that date is tested on the 2025 version. Interviews from either group are happening in the same offices in the same weeks, which is why the two tests keep getting mixed up in study groups and online forums.

What the numbers are

The 2008 test draws on a bank of 100 questions. The officer asks up to 10 of them, and 6 correct answers are a pass.

The 2025 test draws on a bank of 128 questions. The officer asks up to 20, and 12 correct answers are a pass. The questioning stops as soon as the result is settled: at 12 correct the applicant has passed, at 9 wrong the applicant has not, and the officer moves on either way.

That last detail matters more than it looks. On the 2008 test an applicant could afford four wrong answers out of ten. On the 2025 test the margin is eight wrong out of twenty, which sounds generous until you notice the bank is 28 questions larger and the questions themselves are longer.

What stayed the same

Three things did not change.

  • The answers are spoken, not written. The officer asks the question aloud and the applicant answers aloud. There is no multiple choice and no answer sheet.
  • Some questions have several acceptable answers. Naming any one of them is enough. The official materials list the alternatives.
  • A handful of answers depend on where the applicant lives or on who currently holds an office. USCIS updates these on its website, and a booklet printed two years ago may have the wrong name in it.

The 65/20 special consideration also survived the change. An applicant who is 65 or older and has been a lawful permanent resident for at least 20 years studies a list of 20 marked questions, is asked 10 of them, and needs 6 correct. The structure is the same under both versions; what differs is which 20 questions, because the list is drawn from whichever bank applies.

The second chance

Failing the civics test at the first interview is not the end of the application. USCIS schedules a second interview, usually 60 to 90 days later, and retests only the part that was failed. An applicant who passed the English portion does not sit it again.

At the second interview the officer asks a fresh set of questions from the same bank, under the same rules as the first time. A second failure means the application is denied, and starting again requires a new Form N-400 and new fees.

How to tell which test applies

The filing date is printed on the receipt notice, Form I-797C, that USCIS sends after the application is accepted. Anyone preparing from a study guide, a flashcard deck or a class should check that date first and then check that the material matches it.

Preparing from the wrong version is the most common avoidable mistake in this part of the process. The two banks overlap, so the mistake is not obvious: an applicant can study for weeks, recognise most of what comes up, and still meet a run of unfamiliar questions on the day.
