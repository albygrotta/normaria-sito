Hearsay is the rule that students of evidence meet first and understand last. Part of the difficulty is that the everyday meaning of the word, second-hand gossip, is not the legal one. Rule 801 gives a definition with two conditions, and a statement is hearsay only when both are met.

## The definition, in two parts

Under Rule 801(c), a statement is hearsay when the declarant did not make it while testifying at the current trial or hearing, and a party offers it to prove that what the statement asserts is true.

Each half does real work.

The first half explains why a witness on the stand is not a hearsay problem, whatever the witness says. The opposing lawyer can cross-examine, the jury can watch, and the statement was made under oath. A statement made last March in a kitchen offers none of that.

The second half is the one that decides most arguments. A statement offered for some other purpose is not hearsay at all, even if the person who made it is nowhere near the courtroom.

## «Not for the truth» is not a loophole

Lawyers say a statement is offered «not for the truth of the matter asserted», and the phrase sounds like a formula for getting anything in. It is narrower than it sounds, and it describes a real difference.

Consider a landlord sued for ignoring a broken stair. A neighbour testifies that a month before the fall she told the landlord the stair was loose. If the point is that the stair really was loose, the statement is hearsay. If the point is that the landlord knew about it, the statement is not hearsay, because it does not matter whether the stair was loose; what matters is that the words reached him.

The same sentence, the same speaker, two different purposes, two different answers. This is why an evidence question is never finished until the purpose of the offer has been identified.

Other common purposes outside the rule are the effect on the listener, the fact that words were spoken at all when that fact has legal consequences, and the declarant's state of mind shown by the words rather than asserted in them.

## What a statement is

Rule 801(a) defines a statement as an assertion, spoken or written, and also as conduct that the person intended as an assertion. A nod in answer to a question is a statement. Pointing at a photograph when asked who did it is a statement.

Conduct that was not meant to assert anything falls outside. A ship's captain who inspects a vessel and then sails on it with his family is not asserting that the vessel is sound, so his conduct is not a statement, however strongly it suggests the conclusion.

## The categories the rule simply removes

Rule 801(d) takes two groups of statements out of the definition rather than creating an exception for them. The distinction is technical but it shows up in exam questions, which often ask whether something is «not hearsay» or «hearsay but admissible».

The first group is a testifying witness's own earlier statement, where the witness is on the stand and subject to cross-examination, and the earlier statement is one of three kinds: inconsistent with the testimony and made under penalty of perjury at a proceeding or deposition; consistent with the testimony and offered to answer a charge of recent fabrication or to rehabilitate credibility; or an identification of a person the witness perceived earlier.

The second group is an opposing party's statement, offered against that party. It covers the party's own words, words the party adopted or treated as true, words of someone the party authorised to speak, words of an agent or employee on a matter within the job and during it, and words of a coconspirator said during and in furtherance of the conspiracy.

Neither group is admitted because it is reliable. Both are admitted because the usual reason for excluding hearsay, the absence of cross-examination, is missing or because the party can hardly complain about being quoted.

## Then the exceptions

Rule 802 states the bar: hearsay is not admissible unless a rule, a statute or the Supreme Court says otherwise. Rules 803 and 804 then list the exceptions, and the list is long. Present sense impressions, excited utterances, records of a regularly conducted activity and public records are among the ones that come up constantly in practice.

The result is a rule that excludes less than its reputation suggests. The work in a real case is rarely proving that something is hearsay. It is finding which door it comes through.
