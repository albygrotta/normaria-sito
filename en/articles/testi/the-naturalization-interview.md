The naturalization interview has a reputation for being an exam. It is closer to a conversation with a form on the table, and it follows the same order almost every time. Knowing that order removes most of the uncertainty, because an applicant who knows what comes next is not spending the first ten minutes working it out.

## First, the form

The officer begins with Form N-400, the application already on file, and goes through the answers.

Some of the questions are read aloud and confirmed. Others are checked against documents: the permanent resident card, travel history, marital status, children, employment, tax filings, arrests if any. The officer also asks whether anything has changed since the form was filed, and changes are normal. A new job, a new address, a trip abroad, a marriage, all of these are recorded at the interview rather than treated as a problem.

Two kinds of answers cause trouble, and neither is about English. The first is an answer that contradicts the form without explanation. The second is a gap in the travel history, because continuous residence is calculated from those dates.

## The English test, in three parts

The English requirement is tested during the same appointment, in three pieces.

**Speaking.** There is no separate speaking exercise. The officer assesses ordinary spoken English from the interview itself, which is one reason the N-400 review comes first: by the time the test proper begins, the officer has heard the applicant speak for several minutes.

**Reading.** The applicant is shown up to three sentences and must read one of them correctly aloud. Reading one of the three is enough. The vocabulary comes from a published list, and the sentences are built from civics and everyday words.

**Writing.** The officer dictates up to three sentences and the applicant must write one of them correctly. Again, one out of three is enough. Spelling and grammar need not be perfect; the sentence must be understandable and must carry the meaning that was dictated.

## Then the civics test

The civics questions are asked aloud and answered aloud. How many are asked depends on which version of the test applies, and that follows the date Form N-400 was filed: applications filed before 20 October 2025 take the 2008 version, those filed on or after that date take the 2025 version.

Applicants aged 65 or over with at least 20 years as lawful permanent residents are examined on a shorter list of 20 questions and may answer in a language of their choice, bringing their own interpreter.

## If a part goes wrong

Failing one part does not fail the interview.

USCIS schedules a second interview, normally 60 to 90 days after the first, and retests only the part that was not passed. An applicant who passed the English test and failed civics sits civics alone; an applicant who passed civics and failed the writing sentence writes a new sentence and answers no civics questions at all.

A second failure means the application is denied. The process can be started again, but it needs a new Form N-400 and new fees, so the second interview is worth preparing for properly rather than hoping the first result was bad luck.

## What to bring

The appointment notice lists what is required, and the list is short: the notice itself, the permanent resident card, a passport and any travel documents, and whatever the notice specifically asks for.

Beyond that, two documents are worth having even when the notice does not demand them: proof of any name change, and the documents behind any answer on the form that has changed since filing. Producing them at the counter takes a minute. Being asked to send them afterwards adds weeks.
