If you fail part of the naturalization test, what happens next?

November 25, 2026 · 6 min read

A failed portion at the first examination is not a denial. 8 CFR 312.5(a) gives a second opportunity within 90 days of the first examination, and USCIS ordinarily retests only the part that was failed. A missed second appointment counts as a failure. After a second failure the application is denied, and a hearing before a different officer can be requested within 30 days of receiving the notice. Permanent resident status is a separate matter and is not affected.

At the end of the interview the officer says the civics test was not passed. The sentence lands like a verdict. It is not one. A failed portion at the first examination is a step in the process, and the regulations set out exactly what follows.

What is being tested, and in what form

The naturalization test is four separate assessments, and they are passed or failed one by one.

Speaking is not a separate exercise. A USCIS officer judges it during the eligibility interview on Form N-400, from the way the applicant answers questions about the application itself.

Reading and writing are short. The applicant must read one sentence correctly out of three, and write one sentence correctly out of three.

Civics is a set of questions drawn from a published bank. The number asked and the number needed depends on which version of the test applies, and that in turn depends on the filing date of the application rather than the date of the interview. Normaria has a separate article on the two versions now in use.

The second opportunity

The rule is in 8 CFR 312.5(a). An applicant who fails the English literacy test or the history and government test at the first examination is given a second opportunity to pass, within 90 days after the first examination, while the application is still pending.

That is the whole of the written window. Immigration practitioners and study sites commonly describe the second appointment as falling between 60 and 90 days after the first interview, and in practice that is where it usually lands. The 60-day floor is a description of how USCIS schedules, not a term in the regulation. Only the 90-day ceiling is written.

The same is true of a point applicants ask about constantly: whether the second examination covers only the part that was failed. USCIS schedules it that way, and an applicant who passed English at the first interview is ordinarily retested on civics alone. The regulation speaks of a second opportunity to pass "the test or tests" and does not spell this out, so the practice is reliable without being guaranteed in the text.

Missing the second appointment

8 CFR 312.5(b) turns a missed appointment into a failure. An applicant who receives notice of the date of the second examination and does not appear, without good cause and without having told the Service beforehand, is deemed to have failed that second examination.

The notice is the trigger. An address that has changed, or mail that was never collected, does not suspend the rule, which is a strong reason to keep the address on file current until the case is decided.

The clock behind the clock

Two deadlines run alongside each other, and they explain why the 90 days are firm.

USCIS must serve written notice of a denial no later than 120 days after the date of the first examination, under 8 CFR 336.1(a). Because the decision has to issue inside that period, the second examination has to happen inside the 90 days.

When it cannot, 8 CFR 312.5(b) provides the exit. The applicant signs a written waiver of the 120-day requirement, and the Service then has 120 days from the second interview instead. An applicant who asks to postpone is choosing a longer wait for the decision, and should know that before asking.

If the second attempt also fails

The application is denied. The denial concerns naturalization, and it is a separate decision from lawful permanent resident status, which it does not end.

Two routes remain.

The first is a hearing. Under 8 CFR 336.2(a) the applicant, or an authorised representative, may request a hearing on the denial by filing a request with USCIS within 30 days after the applicant receives the notice of denial. The 30 days run from receipt of the notice, not from the date on it. A late request is rejected and the fee is not refunded, unless it meets the standards for a motion to reopen or reconsider.

The hearing is a real review rather than a formality. 8 CFR 336.2(b) requires an officer other than the one who conducted the original examination, classified at a grade equal to or higher than that officer. The reviewing officer may affirm the earlier decision or re-determine it in whole or in part, and may hold a full hearing afresh or use a lighter procedure. The hearing must be scheduled within a reasonable period not exceeding 180 days.

The second route is a new Form N-400, filed when the applicant is ready, with the fee again. The regulations impose no waiting period before refiling.

Who is tested on what

Some applicants sit a different test, and the exemptions turn on two numbers each.

Under 8 CFR 312.1(b)(1), an applicant who on the date of filing is over 50 and has lived in the United States for at least 20 years since lawful admission for permanent residence does not have to demonstrate English. Under 312.1(b)(2), the same applies to an applicant over 55 with at least 15 years.

These exemptions cover English only. 8 CFR 312.2(a) states plainly that an applicant exempt under either provision still has to satisfy the history and government requirement, which may then be taken in the applicant's own language.

Separately, USCIS allows applicants who are 65 or older and have been permanent residents for 20 years or more to study only the questions marked with an asterisk in the official materials. That is a narrower pool, not an exemption.

None of this changes the second-chance rule. An applicant who fails the civics test in their own language under the 50/20 exemption gets the same second examination within 90 days.

Points that are commonly misread

  • A failed portion at the first interview is not a denial. The denial comes after a second failure, or after a missed second appointment.
  • The 90 days are in the regulation. The 60 days are practice.
  • Reading and writing require one correct sentence out of three, not all three.
  • Speaking is assessed through the interview itself, so an applicant who answers the N-400 questions confidently has already addressed that part.
  • The 30 days for a hearing request run from receipt of the denial notice.
  • A denied naturalization application leaves permanent resident status intact.
